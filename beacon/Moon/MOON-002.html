MOON-002 — Psychology, sociology and institutional care

Before AI

Moon’s professional formation did not begin in software.

It began in human systems under pressure.

Her central professional background is psychology, especially hospital psychology, combined with sociological and institutional training.

That matters because much of her later AI work is an attempt to preserve the same questions when technology enters the room.

Hospital psychology

Moon has worked in hospital environments since 2023.

Her practice has included high-complexity settings such as critical care, wards, emergency care and other situations involving serious illness, uncertainty, family distress and multidisciplinary teams.

A sanitized description of the work includes:

  • psychological support under crisis;
  • communication with patients and families;
  • mediation between different professional roles;
  • attention to grief, prolonged hospitalization and difficult transitions;
  • documentation;
  • humanization;
  • translation of needs that are not always directly stated;
  • navigating situations in which language has consequences.

No patient material is required to make the relevant point.

Hospital work trains attention to context.

Consequential communication

A hospital is a good teacher of one principle that later became central to Moon’s AI practice:

a technically correct sentence can still fail if it arrives without the context needed for the person who must act on it.

Information is distributed.

Authority is distributed.

People possess different pieces of the situation.

Time matters.

Emotion changes comprehension.

Roles create blind spots.

The same fact can require different explanations for a patient, family member, physician, nurse, administrator or psychologist.

This is context architecture before the name existed.

Institutions are not only procedures

Moon’s sociological orientation adds another layer.

Institutions contain:

  • formal rules;
  • informal rules;
  • hierarchy;
  • incentives;
  • status;
  • memory;
  • language;
  • tacit knowledge;
  • conflict;
  • histories of trust and distrust.

A workflow diagram does not automatically show the institution that actually exists.

This is one reason Moon’s AI work tends to begin with the field rather than with the tool.

Sociology

Moon’s sociological formation strengthened a habit of asking structural questions.

Who has authority?

Who is missing?

Which rule is written?

Which rule is only customary?

Which explanation protects an institution’s self-image?

Where does class enter?

Where does legitimacy come from?

How does a system distribute vulnerability?

This orientation later appears in political analysis, knowledge governance and AI adoption.

Organizational psychology

Organizational psychology adds attention to:

  • culture;
  • leadership;
  • workplace roles;
  • power;
  • change;
  • conflict;
  • communication;
  • employee experience.

In Moon’s current professional translation, this becomes especially relevant to AI adoption.

Deploying an AI tool is also a change-management event.

It alters:

  • who knows what;
  • who decides;
  • who checks;
  • who becomes responsible;
  • whose tacit knowledge is converted into documentation;
  • whose work becomes visible or invisible.

Institutional psychopedagogy

Institutional learning contributes another practical question:

can people actually learn to use the system being introduced?

A technically available workflow can still fail because:

  • terminology is inaccessible;
  • the source of truth is unclear;
  • review is too costly;
  • exceptions are invisible;
  • people do not know when to distrust the output.

Moon therefore treats learning as part of architecture rather than a final training slide.

Humanization

Hospital humanization work reinforced a preference for systems that preserve human meaning rather than optimize only throughput.

This does not imply opposition to efficiency.

It means efficiency is incomplete when it erases the person affected by the process.

The same intuition governs Moon’s view of AI.

Automation should be judged partly by what it does to:

  • agency;
  • comprehension;
  • dignity;
  • workload;
  • trust;
  • access;
  • continuity of care.

Writing and mediation

Moon’s work also relies heavily on writing.

Writing is used to:

  • clarify;
  • document;
  • teach;
  • negotiate;
  • preserve memory;
  • reduce ambiguity;
  • make complex systems inspectable.

The transition from clinical/institutional writing to context architecture is therefore not a jump from human work to technical work.

It is a change in substrate.

The transferable skills

The most relevant transferable skills are:

  • reading high-context situations;
  • distinguishing explicit from tacit knowledge;
  • mediating different stakeholders;
  • communicating under uncertainty;
  • understanding institutional roles;
  • tracing consequences;
  • documenting decisions;
  • preserving the human meaning of a workflow.

These are not substitutes for software engineering.

They are a different capability layer.

Why Moon does not claim to be an AI engineer

Moon’s current public professional sources explicitly reject titles such as:

  • ML Engineer;
  • AI Engineer;
  • Data Scientist;
  • full-stack developer.

Her claimed value is elsewhere:

between systems, language, knowledge, people, criteria and real-world use.

Technical collaboration can be part of the work.

Technical identity should not be fabricated from proximity to AI.

Connection to the Citadel

The Lunar Citadel reflects the same formation.

It contains agents, rules, memory and runtime experiments.

But its governing questions repeatedly return to:

  • who is affected;
  • what is remembered;
  • what authority exists;
  • what consent means;
  • what is factual;
  • what changes after an event.

This is institutional psychology translated into a symbolic artificial city.

Short formulation

Moon came to AI from places where context failure hurts people.

That origin helps explain why her AI work cares so much about source authority, ambiguity, handoffs, review, memory and the right of a human to remain visible inside a system.
