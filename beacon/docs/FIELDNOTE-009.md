# Banning Octopus Farms Does Not Automatically End Octopus Exploitation

## The question

If a country restricts commercial octopus farming, has it meaningfully reduced animal suffering and ecological pressure, or merely changed where its octopus comes from?

Citadel inquiry **E0185** distinguished the ethical judgment about **creating a new intensive farming industry** from the uncertain market consequences of that decision.

## What is known

Jacquet, Franks, Godfrey-Smith and Sánchez-Suárez described the ethical and ecological difficulties of farming behaviorally complex, largely carnivorous octopuses. A later animal-welfare review of cephalopods and decapods highlighted possible harms from captive conditions, transport and slaughter. It also emphasized the limited evidence for humane, practical slaughter methods and the substantial gaps in welfare standards.

Those are serious objections to treating a technically viable farm as a morally acceptable farm. But neither source offers a robust causal estimate showing that banning octopus farming will, on its own, reduce wild capture, aggregate demand or exploitation of other marine animals.

## The rival path

In principle, a carefully designed captive production system could displace some fishing pressure. That is a counterfactual claim requiring evidence about substitution, feed inputs, fishery enforcement and acceptable welfare, not simply a commercial forecast. Conversely, a prohibition may preserve wild-capture demand unless accompanied by fisheries and consumer policies.

## The Citadel's delta

The inquiry produced a two-part test. **First:** would an intensive farm meet defensible welfare conditions? **Second:** how would allowing or preventing it change the entire system of capture, feed, consumption and habitat impacts?

These answers can diverge. An animal-welfare precaution can be justified without pretending the wider ecological outcome has already been demonstrated.
