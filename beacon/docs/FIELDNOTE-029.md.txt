# Helping a Student Finish a Problem Is Not the Same as Teaching It

## The question

Does access to an AI tutor improve learning, or merely make students better at completing exercises while the tool is available?

Citadel inquiry **E0206** raised a useful question about access restrictions, but its brief consolidated formulation was **too coarse**. The strongest retrieved experiment does not support a blanket claim that unrestricted access is always better for learning.

## What the experiment actually measured

A field experiment with nearly **1,000 high-school mathematics students**, published in *PNAS* in 2025, compared access to a relatively unrestricted GPT-based assistant, a tutor with pedagogical safeguards, and learning without those tools. Both AI conditions improved performance on tasks during AI-assisted practice, but when AI was removed, students in the less constrained GPT condition performed worse than those who had never had access. The guarded tutor was designed to provide hints rather than simply complete work.

A separate 2026 short-course experiment with **42 programming learners** found better pre/post learning improvements with a more guided assistant than with an unrestricted chatbot. This smaller study is suggestive, not a generalization to every curriculum.

## The strongest objection

Blocking access can prevent useful feedback and disproportionately affect students who lack human tutoring. Meanwhile, thoughtfully designed tools can support explanation, guided practice and reflection. Neither study supports a universal prescription that every student should be prohibited from using AI or that all forms of tutoring are interchangeable.

## The interpretive delta

The editorial correction to E0206 is explicit: **the meaningful dimension is not simply permission versus prohibition, but the relation between assisted performance and retained independent competence**. Evaluation should test what students can do after support is removed.
