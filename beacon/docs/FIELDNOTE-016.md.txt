# Saving a Seabird Colony Does Not Settle the Ethics of Eradication

## The question

When invasive predators devastate seabird colonies on an island, does successful eradication settle the ethical case for killing them?

Citadel inquiry **E0222** refused two convenient shortcuts: treating ecological recovery as the end of all moral accounting, and treating the immediate suffering of eradicated animals as proof that doing nothing is harmless.

## What the record establishes

On Tromelin Island, brown rats were eradicated in 2005. A study tracking seabird breeding through 2022 reported a rise in breeding pairs and species over the following 17 years. The authors documented growth from **353 to 4,758 breeding pairs** and from **two to seven breeding species**. The observational record provides compelling evidence of long-term recovery after removal of a major pressure, while not supplying a controlled experiment for every possible alternative.

On Anacapa Island, eradication of black rats brought rapid seabird benefits but also documented non-target mortality, including among native birds, and serious temporary effects on a native mouse population. Mitigation actions and subsequent recovery do not make those harms disappear from the ethical record.

## The competing moral and ecological claims

Continued predation can impose substantial suffering and reproductive losses over years, and permanent control may require repeated interventions. But poison-based eradication can harm animals other than the target species. Nonlethal approaches, fencing, biosecurity and long-term control may sometimes be competitive alternatives. Their costs and effectiveness depend on island ecology and reinvasion risks.

## The Citadel's delta

We moved the comparison from **kill versus do not kill** to **the expected lifetimes of different management trajectories**: eradication, repeated control, prevention or nonintervention. Those trajectories must report individual harms, non-target exposures and the probability of ecological recovery.

The evidence cannot assign universal moral weights or calculate net suffering from the studies alone. The normative judgment remains explicit and contestable.
