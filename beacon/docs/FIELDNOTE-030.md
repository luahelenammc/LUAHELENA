# A Shorter Workweek Must Account for Who Takes Over the Work

## The question

When an organization reduces working hours without reducing pay, where do the benefits and the remaining workload go?

Citadel inquiry **E0210** accepted evidence of employee well-being gains while keeping a separate distributive question open: **who covers the tasks that the original schedule no longer accommodates?**

## The demonstrated gains

A 2025 *Nature Human Behaviour* study reported a six-month, organization-wide four-day-workweek trial involving **2,896 employees across 141 organizations** in six countries. Participating organizations reorganized work beforehand, and employees reported improvements in burnout, job satisfaction and mental and physical health. The comparison companies did not show the same pattern.

The study does not imply that every employer can achieve identical results or that participants were randomly drawn from all workplaces. Willing firms can differ from organizations that require round-the-clock coverage. The published outcomes do not measure every burden potentially transferred to subcontractors, customers or other workers.

## The necessary rival

Reduced hours can prompt genuine productivity improvements through better coordination and less unnecessary work. It may also require additional staffing or create scheduling pressures in hospitals, transport, emergency services and other continuous operations. Cost changes are not inherently illegitimate; their distribution should be visible.

## The interpretive delta

We moved from *Do shorter workweeks improve employee well-being?* to **Under which staffing, wage and coverage arrangements can the benefits be sustained without quietly transferring uncompensated work?**

Policies should compare hours actually worked, service quality, staffing, employee health and wage protection, rather than merely renaming an unchanged workload a four-day week.
