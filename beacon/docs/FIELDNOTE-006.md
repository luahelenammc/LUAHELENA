# Housing Is Not a Prize for Getting Well

## The question

Should someone first demonstrate clinical recovery or abstinence before a public program offers stable housing? Or is that requirement withholding one of the conditions that may make recovery possible?

Lunar Citadel inquiry **E0128** challenged a common institutional sequence: treatment first, housing later. The important distinction is not between *housing* and *care*, but between **housing as a conditionally earned reward** and **housing with care that does not disappear after relapse**.

## What the research supports

In the Canadian *At Home/Chez Soi* randomized program, housing subsidies combined with support services improved housing stability for people experiencing homelessness and mental illness compared with usual services. A 2015 JAMA trial documented results among participants with moderate support needs. Longer-term follow-up showed durable gains in stable housing, while outcomes varied across mental health and other measures.

A four-year French randomized-trial follow-up similarly found higher housing stability, better autonomy and fewer inpatient days in Housing First participants, but did not find uniformly superior recovery or symptom outcomes. These are not trials of keys to an apartment without services: subsidies, case management, clinical availability and institutional capacity formed part of the interventions.

## The resistance

A stable home does not treat every psychiatric condition, prevent all harms, or eliminate the need for sustained care. Housing First can require substantial public expenditure and competent long-term support. A claim that it is effective for housing stability should not become an unsupported claim that it automatically resolves substance dependence or mental illness.

## The Citadel's interpretive delta

We moved the gate of eligibility: **the right to remain housed should not depend on the immediate success of treatment**. This is a design principle informed by specific randomized programs, not an original clinical trial or a universal cost-benefit conclusion.

The sharper policy question is which mix of housing stability, voluntary care and sustained public capacity produces durable outcomes without treating relapse as grounds for losing one's home.
