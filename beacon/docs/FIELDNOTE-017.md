# More Spawns Are Not the Same as More Viable Offspring

## The question

Does eyestalk ablation increase shrimp production, or does measuring only a selected reproductive outcome disguise costs to the breeding animals?

Citadel inquiry **E0225** revisited the practice of removing or damaging an eyestalk to stimulate ovarian maturation. It distinguished **spawning frequency** from **cumulative viable offspring across an animal's productive life**, with survival and welfare considered separately.

## Evidence that resists a single answer

A study of commercial shrimp hatcheries in Vietnam examined reproductively mature *Penaeus vannamei* and *P. monodon* with and without ablation. In a 2024 *P. vannamei* setting, non-ablated females produced substantially more nauplii per female over the observed commercial period. Yet the same paper reported very poor reproduction without ablation for *P. monodon* under the conditions studied. The relevant contrast is not merely treated versus untreated, but also species, domestication, husbandry and the period of observation.

An earlier Brazilian commercial-hatchery study of *P. vannamei* found favorable reproductive performance in non-ablated females and greater mortality in the ablated group. These specific findings argue against presenting ablation as a technically unavoidable universal practice.

## The strongest rival

For some species and hatchery conditions, ablation can still substantially affect maturation and larval output. A laboratory or farm comparison does not establish that one replacement method works everywhere. Welfare evidence also cannot be reduced to a single reproductive count: stress responses, injury, survival and behavioral needs matter.

## The Citadel's interpretive delta

The metric changed from **how often a female spawns** to **what cumulative production, animal condition and survival look like through the entire breeding trajectory**. A farming decision must report its biological and welfare costs, not only its most favorable production indicator.

## A better comparison

Which non-ablative strategies preserve lifetime viable output while reducing injury and mortality, and how does that answer change by species and hatchery?
