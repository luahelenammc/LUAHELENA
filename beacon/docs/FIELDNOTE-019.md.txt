# A River Can Feed Wells Before the Aquifer Looks Empty

## The question

How can an aquifer under stress reduce the water flowing in a river, even while groundwater still appears available to pump?

Citadel inquiry **E0118** separated the visible persistence of wells from the less visible connection between groundwater levels and river flow.

## What the measurements show

A 2024 *Nature Communications* study examined water-level records from **17,972 wells** across Brazil that met its selection criteria. In **55.4%** of these wells, water levels were lower than the adjacent river-water surface. That geometry indicates potential for river water to move into the subsurface. It does **not** measure the flow rate at every river reach or establish pumping as the cause of every gradient.

Some rivers naturally lose water to aquifers because of geology or climate. Pumping can alter gradients and reduce groundwater contributions to streams, often with delayed effects. The study's lack of fine-scale subsurface geology limits direct causal attribution and basin-level quantification.

## An important objection

Not every river-to-aquifer exchange is environmental degradation; some are normal and valuable components of water recharge. Reduced pumping will not necessarily restore streamflow rapidly, and effects vary by aquifer properties, hydrological conditions and competing water demands.

## The interpretive delta

The management unit cannot end at the wellhead. **Groundwater permits and surface-water allocation govern connected reservoirs**, even when their administrative registries are separate. Measuring extraction volumes alone misses where the pumped water would otherwise have flowed over time.

An adequate policy test compares dry-season streamflow, groundwater levels and modeled counterfactual extraction, while reporting uncertainty about geology and climate.
