# The Water That an Efficient Farm Did Not Save

## The question

If a farm requires less irrigation water per unit of crop, has its river basin actually gained water?

Consolidated Citadel inquiry **E0216** examined the difference between on-farm efficiency and basin-level conservation. The central shift was from measuring water delivered to measuring **water depleted**, including the effects of farmers' choices and allocation rules.

## Why the scale matters

The 2018 *Science* article *The paradox of irrigation efficiency*, by Grafton and colleagues, challenged the assumption that public subsidies for better irrigation hardware reliably free water for ecosystems or other users. Water not applied to a field can be a real local benefit, but some of the previous irrigation water may have returned to streams or groundwater. Removing a recoverable return flow is not the same as reducing basin-wide consumption.

The authors also point to behavioral feedback: farmers may expand irrigated area or intensify production when water use becomes more productive. In that case, a more efficient system may leave total depletion unchanged or even increase it.

But the paradox is not a universal ban on efficiency improvements. A 2023 analysis by B. A. Lankford argues that the common formulation can overemphasize return flows or obscure other controlled variables. When withdrawals, acreage, crop evapotranspiration and reuse are accounted for together, **real savings can be engineered and reallocated**.

## Our interpretive delta

The relevant question is not merely whether the irrigation device is efficient. It is **who can claim the water after efficiency changes**, whether extraction is capped, and whether the accounting follows the river basin rather than the farm boundary.

That distinction makes governance and hydrology inseparable: an engineering investment without water rights, enforcement and basin-scale measurement cannot alone establish an environmental gain.

## The best counterargument

Technological changes can materially reduce losses in some systems, and policy can deliberately prevent rebound. The sign and magnitude of the effect vary with the basin, soil, crops, irrigation practice and regulation. No single conclusion can be inferred from the word *efficiency*.

## A sharper public test

After an efficiency subsidy, how much **net depletion** decreased across the basin, and who was entitled to the remainder?
