# When the Color of Turmeric Becomes a Poisoning Incentive

## The question

What happens when consumers and traders treat a vivid yellow color as proof of quality, even though a toxic pigment can produce that signal?

Citadel inquiry **E0120** interpreted turmeric adulteration as a problem of **information and market incentives**, not merely a laboratory failure to detect lead.

## A documented intervention

Researchers investigating supply chains in Bangladesh found that lead chromate, a yellow pigment, was used to improve turmeric's appearance. Their 2019 study described producer incentives and evidence of adulteration in several producing districts. A 2023 *Environmental Research* follow-up assessed a multi-part intervention involving research communication, market education, rapid detection and regulatory enforcement.

In the studied market samples, detectable lead fell from **47% in 2019 to 0% in 2021**. The intervention report also found reduced evidence of pigment use at sampled mills. These results are consequential, but **zero detections in a defined sample are not proof of nationwide eradication**. Because the intervention had several simultaneous elements, their separate causal effects were not experimentally identified.

## A necessary objection

Lead can enter food and blood through several pathways; finding lead alone does not prove deliberate pigment adulteration in a specific case. Other countries have different supply chains. Nothing in these Bangladesh studies establishes the prevalence of turmeric adulteration in Brazil.

## The interpretive delta

A successful response must change the incentives around the *signal of quality*: reliable inspection and penalties are important, but buyers also need a way to value verified safety rather than bright appearance. The right outcome is durable reduction of exposure and recurrence, not simply cleaner samples during the campaign.
