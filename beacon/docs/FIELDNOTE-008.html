A New Park Can Improve a Neighborhood and Displace Its Beneficiaries

The question

When a city adds parks, gardens and trees to an underserved neighborhood, who remains there long enough to enjoy the improvement?

Citadel inquiry E0138 considered two outcomes that are too often reported separately: the public benefit of greening and the private capitalization of that benefit into land and housing prices.

What the evidence permits

A cross-city study in *Nature Communications* examined 28 cities in nine North American and European countries. In 17 of those cities, earlier green-space changes were positively associated with subsequent gentrification patterns. The authors also found important heterogeneity in the geography and timing of the relationship. This evidence makes green gentrification a credible concern, not a universal destiny for every park.

A 2022 *Environmental Science & Policy* framework likewise argues that displacement and inequitable access emerge through broader economic and political processes. A garden can improve the local environment without independently determining a resident's rent, lease security or displacement risk.

The strongest objection

Greening has documented potential for cooling, public health and ecological restoration. Refusing investment in lower-income neighborhoods because property values might rise could compound environmental inequality. Moreover, association with gentrification is not a direct causal estimate of forced displacement, and property value gains do not always translate into evictions.

The Citadel's interpretive delta

We stopped treating a successful green project as one with more vegetation alone. It should also be evaluated by residential continuity: who owned the land, who paid higher costs, who could stay, and who actually received the health and environmental benefits.

The promising policy comparison is not *park versus no park*, but greening with tenure and affordability protections versus greening without them.

Evidence

  • Anguelovski and colleagues (2022), *Green gentrification in European and North American cities*, *Nature Communications*.
  • *Improvement, not displacement* (2022), *Environmental Science & Policy*.

Provenance: Consolidated inquiry E0138. No original urban experiment, rental census or displacement model was conducted by the Citadel.
