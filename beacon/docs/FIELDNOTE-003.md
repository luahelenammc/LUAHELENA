# When Participation Expands but Authority Concentrates

## The question

Can a participatory institution include people historically excluded from politics and still concentrate effective authority inside its own representative structures?

Lunar Citadel inquiry **E0213** shifted attention from the social composition of participants to the changing relationship between participants and representatives. This is a public interpretation of published research, not a field investigation conducted by the Citadel.

## Evidence that complicates the usual success measure

Fedozzi and Martins' 2015 study of participatory budgeting in Porto Alegre, Brazil, found that an institution could retain a socially inclusive participant profile while signs of political elitization emerged in representation. The authors examined accountability, knowledge of participation rules and rules governing representatives' mandates. Their analysis identified growing informational asymmetries and decisions about re-election made by representatives despite disagreement among participating constituents.

The pattern is not confined to one city or necessarily identical elsewhere. A 2017 study of New York City's participatory budgeting examined changes in its cross-district steering committee and reported that expansion and administrative adaptation created real gains but also constrained elements of community empowerment.

The first distinction, then, is between **access to the forum** and **power to alter its rules**. A larger, more representative gathering does not by itself tell us whether ordinary participants can correct, replace or overrule an entrenched leadership.

## An objection that matters

Specialization is not automatically capture. Experienced delegates can preserve institutional memory and translate complex budgets into implementable decisions. Holdo's study of participatory budgeting in Rosario also shows why co-optation is not inevitable: incentives and institutional relationships can sustain participants' independence.

Nor did the Porto Alegre study establish a universal law of oligarchization. A single case cannot tell us how frequently this tension arises in every participatory design.

## Our interpretive delta

The question changed from **Who gets into the room?** to **Who can revise the rules after the room fills?** A better audit would examine mandate limits, the ability to contest delegate decisions, access to procedural information and the path from deliberation to binding resource allocation.

## The unresolved test

Which decision about the institution itself can ordinary participants genuinely reverse, and how would we know?
